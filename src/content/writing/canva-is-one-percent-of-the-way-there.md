*Three years inside the magic factory that grew up around Melanie Perkins.*

Canva is a harness, and Melanie Perkins is the model inside it.

If you've built anything with LLMs recently the shape is familiar. The model makes the call. The harness is everything around it: the context you feed it, the tools it can reach, the loops that let it see what happened and try again. You don't improve the system by wishing for a bigger model. You improve it by building a harness that gets the model the right context at the right moment and then lets it act on the world.

Canva has been running that architecture on a person since long before anyone had a word for it. Paul Graham named half of it in [September 2024](https://paulgraham.com/foundermode.html), by which point Canva was eleven years in, and the half with the name is the easy half. The half nobody writes about is the machine that has to exist around a founder for any of it to produce something other than chaos. I think it's the most underrated piece of org design in tech.

I was there for three years. I joined in March 2023 to run Discovery, which meant search and recommendations, and spent most of a year restructuring those teams so they could incubate what became a big part of [Magic Studio](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/magic-studio/). In June 2024 I became the founding head of product for a new generative AI group that pulled AI Experiences, AI Research, and AI Platform under one roof, and we shipped [Canva AI](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/canva-create-2025/). In January 2026 I moved into a product lead role to launch [Canva AI 2.0](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/canva-create-2026-ai/). I left in May.

Three roles and three org shapes in three years, all pointed at the same problem. I'll come back to why that's the most important thing I have to say.

## It should not have worked

You've probably read the origin story. Perth, which Cliff Obrecht describes as [the most isolated city in the world](https://techcrunch.com/2022/09/13/canva-moves-beyond-graphic-design-to-launch-a-visual-worksuite/), where he says they didn't know what venture capital was. [More than a hundred investor rejections](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-making-of-canva). Mel [learning to kitesurf](https://www.npr.org/2019/01/24/688299882/canva-melanie-perkins) to get in front of an investor who ran his networking events at kitesurfing retreats. Two things came out of it.

The first is profitability, because when capital is hard to get you build something that doesn't need it. Canva has now been [profitable for eight straight years](https://sacra.com/c/canva/) at a growth rate where almost nobody is. That isn't thrift. Canva spends hard when it decides to. What profitability buys is independence from everyone else's clock, and the clearest proof is a bet that almost never comes up when people write about Canva: the company once spent [two years rewriting its entire codebase](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-making-of-canva) and shipped almost no new features while it happened. You can only do that if nobody in the room is running on a runway. It's also why you can't win an argument at Canva by pointing at what a competitor just raised. Arguments get won by pointing at users.

The second is a habit of deriving things from scratch. Being half a day and seven thousand miles from Silicon Valley doesn't make you provincial. It makes you uninterested in calibrating yourself to whatever a room in San Francisco currently considers normal, which is a different thing and a much more valuable one.

You can watch this at the end of a Canva keynote. In 2024 the company closed Canva Create with a rap battle. [Fifty million people saw it inside 48 hours](https://www.fastcompany.com/91313954/canva-create-musical-performance) and a decent share of tech Twitter called it cringe. The following year Canva came back with a 42-person cast, a live band, a gospel choir, and a song whose lyrics were real feature requests from the community. The choice, as it was put internally, was to shrink and play it safe or be unapologetically ourselves.

There is a great deal of costume. People are cheerful in a way that might take an American a while to trust. Underneath the cheerfulness is the hardest-working group of people I've been around. I started calling it laid-back intensity and I still don't have a better phrase. Nobody is performing stress. Everybody is moving extremely fast.

## One percent of the way there

In 2011, before there was a company, Mel made a deck for something called [Canvas Chef](https://nbt.substack.com/p/canva-original-deck). The pitch was that you could describe an idea and have it appear, designed, without knowing anything about design. Fifteen years later she stood on stage at Canva Create and [quoted her own deck back to the room](https://www.forbes.com.au/news/innovation/canva-create-2026-melanie-perkins-unveils-canva-ai-2-0-and-claude-design-deal/) while launching an AI that does exactly that. Canva is the only place I've worked where a document written before the company existed still functions as a spec. When thousands of people can predict what the founder would say about a decision, they stop needing to ask her about every one, and she gets to choose where to be involved rather than being the bottleneck everywhere. A vision that specific behaves less like a slogan and more like compression.

*From the original 2011 Canvas Chef deck, via [NBT's archive of the pitch](https://nbt.substack.com/p/canva-original-deck).*

It also never lets you arrive. The phrase used inside the company is that we're one percent of the way there, and Mel has [said a version of it publicly](https://leaders.com/articles/leaders-stories/melanie-perkins/): a company is still a startup while its dreams are much bigger than its size. What makes it more than a slogan is that the number never moves. People who were there in the early days say she was using it at a few hundred employees. She was still using it at a quarter of a billion users and eight profitable years.

We launched [Canva AI](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/canva-create-2025/), which was the entire reason the group I ran had been assembled. It went well. Within what felt like no time the conversation had stopped being about an assistant and started being about an agent that does the whole job, remembers you between sessions, and keeps working while you're offline, which is roughly [what we announced a year later](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/canva-create-2026-ai/) as Canva AI 2.0. The target had moved before I'd finished being pleased about hitting the last one.

That should be exhausting, and mostly it isn't, because the expansion is genuine rather than rhetorical. If the objective is to empower the world to design, then the world is large and design is most things, and there is no version of the job where you're finished. Mel holds ambition at that size without ever letting it curdle into fantasy, and I haven't seen anyone else manage it.

## The blank design

Mel is in the details, all of them, and so is Cliff. The founder mode conversation gets stuck on whether a founder should be that involved, which is the boring half of it. Plenty of founders are deep in the details and produce nothing but chaos. What's worth studying at Canva is what the involvement produces, and what the company then does with it.

On Canva AI 2.0 we were designing memory for the agent. What we brought to the review was a good version of the thing you've already seen elsewhere: the assistant remembers facts about you and states them back as a list. We'd tried to make it ours by making it visual, so it would hold your color preferences and photos of the people you design about most often.

Mel's objection wasn't that it was wrong. It was that it was techie. It was memory the way engineers understand memory, dressed up a bit.

So she opened a blank Canva design and started drawing.

What she prototyped in the room was a library. Not a list of remembered facts, but a shelf of books organized by category: the big events in your life, the campaigns you run, the things that are simply true about you. Same underlying capability, entirely different mental model. Then she pushed it into the onboarding, where a single button would read everything you had ever made in Canva and write an About Me document to seed the library on your first day.

You can go and look at it. It shipped as the [Memory Library](https://www.canva.com/help/memory-library/), and the first book in it is called About Me. The thing she drew on a blank canvas in a product review made it all the way to launch with its mental model completely intact, which almost never happens to anything.

That instinct is the one I most want to keep. Memory in LLM products is an insider concept and it almost always ships as one. Mel's was to find the mental model a person already carries around, a library and a shelf and a book, and put the new capability inside it. When no existing model fits she builds one and makes it feel obvious in retrospect. She also didn't write a brief, ask for three options, or send it back for another round. She made the artifact herself, in the product, in front of the people who were going to build it.

Here's the part I've been leaving out. A team had spent weeks on the design we brought into that room. It was good work. Ten minutes later it wasn't the plan anymore.

That happens at Canva more than anywhere else I've worked, and it is hard on people. Not because anyone's ego is bruised. Because the thing that keeps a team fast is believing that the work they're doing now is the work that ships, and every time the founder redraws something that belief takes a small hit. Do it enough times without care and you get a team that hedges, that holds back its best thinking, that waits to find out what the real plan is before committing.

Absorbing that is most of the job for everyone sitting between Mel and the team. Not by softening the decision, which wastes it. By making sure the team sees the reasoning and not just the outcome, fast enough that nobody spends a week wondering whether their last month counted. When it goes well people come out of a review like that energized, because the new idea is obviously better and they can see why. When it goes badly you have quietly taught a group of very good people that their judgment doesn't matter.

That's the real cost of building a company this way, and the founder doesn't pay it. The layers underneath her do. Most of the writing about founder mode skips this part, which may be because most of it is written by founders.

## What the harness actually is

Rituals exist so Mel sees the right work at the right stage. Reviews are structured so she's looking at the real artifact rather than a description of it. Teams are shaped so the surface she touches is the surface where her judgment adds the most, and everything else runs without her.

The rule I'd point at first is that whoever built the thing is the one who presents it. Mel wants the person whose hands were on the work in the room, talking, not a director summarizing three levels of remove. That sounds like a small preference about meeting etiquette. It isn't. It's the difference between judgment arriving as a relayed instruction and judgment arriving as a conversation with the person who can act on it, and it's most of why the redraws land as energizing rather than demoralizing. The person whose design got replaced heard the reasoning first-hand, from her, in the moment.

Canva reorganizes when the latency between Mel's judgment and the work gets too high. Not when a leader leaves, not when headcount crosses a number, not because the planning cycle came round again. When the loop gets slow, the org changes shape and the person doesn't. Three roles in three years, all pointed at the same problem, and I never once thought the reorgs were arbitrary.

I've been using the word harness about the company. It also describes what we were building. Canva's product is a design tool, the agent is a designer, and the harness is the canvas, the brand kit, the content library, the multiplayer document. The hard calls on Canva AI were never about which model to use. They were about which surfaces give an agent enough context to be useful and enough constraint to not be dangerous. The model rotates and the harness doesn't, and it took me two years to notice that the sentence I'd write about our product was the sentence I'd write about the company building it.

## The twenty slides

We had Canva AI 2.0 working end to end and had moved into the part of a project where you stop adding and start raising the quality bar. Mel came to the review with a twenty-page deck. One issue per slide. Some were fundamental problems at the level of patterns and some were a specific spacing value on a specific surface, and she had found every one of them herself by being the biggest power user of the product.

The one I think about most was the Canva AI logo in the sidebar. The gap around it, and the way the animation came in as you hovered, didn't feel right to her. She could not be talked out of it. She went and worked with the brand design team through dozens of versions of that hover state before we shipped one.

Our design lead took that deck and used it to stand up a dedicated polish workstream, some of our best engineers on it, running for the final months before launch and doing nothing but end-to-end product quality. Twenty slides of complaints didn't become a list of tickets assigned to whoever had capacity. They became a team.

She has the highest craft bar I've worked under and she is unambiguously the hardest-working person I've met, which in most companies produces a specific kind of fear. It doesn't there. Feedback at Canva is [constant and unarmored](https://ethics.org.au/how-the-canva-crew-learned-to-love-feedback/), and it works because nobody believes the standard is about them. She isn't hard on people. She's hard on the work, and she's hardest on her own. The people who do well there match that shape: user-obsessed builders who prototype rather than describe, who will go into the details, and who carry very little ego about whose idea won. The [values](https://www.lifeatcanva.com/en/our-culture/) function as real screening criteria rather than wall decoration, which I have not seen anywhere since.

## Cliff

None of this holds without Cliff.

A company organized to move one person's judgment quickly will amplify all of it, which means the second voice in the room has to be genuinely independent. Cliff is one of the best operators I have ever seen, and he is the reason the harness never became an echo chamber. He got into tech more or less by accident, he's relaxed to the point of being disarming, and he will say the thing he actually thinks in front of everyone, including sometimes when it is the opposite of what Mel just said. They are married, they have built a [$40 billion company](https://sacra.com/c/canva/) together, and they will still argue about a feature in front of the team without either of them softening it. Most executive teams sand that down before it reaches the room, and I think they're worse for it. The disagreement is the signal. If the team never sees it, they never learn how the decision was actually made.

Three separate times while I was there, a senior leader left and Cliff took over running their organization himself. Not as an interim placeholder while a search ran. He actually ran it, for as long as it took, on the theory that you can't tell whether a group is pointed at the right thing until you've been inside its machinery, and you can't hire the right person to lead it until you know what the job really is. He's also where the pressure on dates comes from, and the pushing is nearly always a prioritization argument wearing a schedule costume. If the date won't move, the team has to decide what actually matters, and most of the value is in what gets cut.

The part I'll miss is the format. A real strategic disagreement would arrive as a considered document laying out his thinking, with screenshots of the examples that got him there. A quality problem would arrive as the screenshot and one line, usually along the lines of: *this is crap, lol.*

That is a better way to run a company than it looks. The document tells you he did the work himself before asking you to do more of it. The "lol" tells you it isn't about you. And between them you know within about four seconds exactly where you stand, which is more than most executives manage in a quarter.

## Step two

Canva has what it calls a [two-step plan](https://www.canva.com/step-two/). Step one is to become one of the most valuable companies in the world. Step two is to do the most good it can.

It was originally understood as a sequence, and the thing Mel worked out early is that it isn't one. The two steps feed each other. The mission is a large part of why the best people join and stay, which makes the company more valuable, which makes step two bigger. Treating them as phases means step two never arrives, because there is always a reason to keep building first.

Mel and Cliff signed the Giving Pledge in 2021 and have committed [more than 80% of their wealth](https://www.forbes.com.au/news/leadership/inside-billionaire-canva-cofounders-plan-to-give-away-their-fortune/), including 30% of the company, to the Canva Foundation. The largest thing it funds is a [$150 million commitment](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/give-directly-update-2025/) to hand cash directly, with no conditions on how it's spent, to people in extreme poverty in Malawi, run through GiveDirectly since 2021. The [early results](https://www.givedirectly.org/district-scale) are hard to argue with: in one sub-district where every adult was paid, 90% of recipients were above the extreme poverty line three months later, child mortality fell 48%, and school enrolment rose 23%. Unconditional cash transfers are about the least self-flattering way to give money away that exists. No building gets your name on it.

The external skeptic might read this as PR. From inside it changes the arithmetic. Every dollar of enterprise value you help create is partly dry powder aimed at problems that actually matter, rather than a marginal increase in the net worth of a small number of people who already have enough. That's a materially different reason to work hard. It's why I stayed as long as I did, and I don't think I was unusual.

## Why I left, and why it's about to be everyone's problem

I was in California. Everyone I worked with was in Sydney. For three years that was manageable. What changed is that the loop got faster. Coding agents compressed the distance between an idea and a working version of it far enough that the company started re-centralizing around the speed of its own iteration, and a seventeen-hour gap stops being a scheduling inconvenience and starts being a place where decisions happen without you. The harness ran faster than I could be inside it. I don't say that as a complaint. It's the strongest evidence I have that the thing I've described here is real.

It's also the shape of what's coming for everyone else. For most of software history the bottleneck was building, and everything we know about running product organizations was learned under that constraint. That constraint is dissolving. When a working version of an idea takes an afternoon instead of a quarter, throughput stops being gated on engineering capacity and starts being gated on how fast good judgment can reach the work, and how much of it there is.

Which is the problem Canva has been solving, without meaning to, since 2013. Not because anyone saw agents coming, but because they had one person with an unusually clear picture of the future and spent a decade building the machinery to get that picture in front of the right work at the right moment. They optimized for judgment latency in an era when everyone else could afford not to.

A lot of companies are about to reach for founder mode and get the visible half. The founder in the details, the reviews, the redrawn designs. That half is easy and it mostly produces chaos. The harness is the work, it takes years, and nobody writes about it because it isn't a personality.

## What I'm taking

Prototype the idea instead of describing it. A blank design and ten minutes beat a brief, every time.

Decide what the money is for before you have any of it, because if you wait for step one to finish, step one never does.

Most writing about tech wealth assumes that accumulating it and doing good with it are in tension, and that the second is an apology for the first. The two-step plan makes a harder claim: they run on the same engine. I watched that work for three years at close range and I'm no longer willing to treat it as a contradiction. Capitalism is a machine for concentrating resources. What almost nobody does is decide, early and in writing, what the concentrated resources are for.

There's no way to install a Melanie Perkins. But the harness can be copied, so can the ambition, and the arithmetic of step two is available to anyone willing to commit to it while it still costs something.

Mel would say we're one percent of the way there. She was saying it long before I arrived and she said it again after we shipped the thing we'd spent two years building, and for a long time I heard it as a way of keeping people hungry. I don't hear it that way anymore. It's the most accurate sentence anyone at that company says.
