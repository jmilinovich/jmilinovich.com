We're one percent of the way there. It's the phrase you hear most inside Canva, said casually and meant completely. Mel Perkins was using it when the company was a few hundred people. She was still using it at a quarter of a billion users and eight profitable years.

I was there for three of those years. I joined in March 2023 to run Discovery, and spent most of a year restructuring search and recommendations so they could incubate what became a big part of [Magic Studio](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/magic-studio/). In June 2024 I became the founding head of product for a new generative AI group, and we shipped [Canva AI](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/canva-create-2025/). In January 2026 I moved into a product lead role to launch [Canva AI 2.0](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/canva-create-2026-ai/). I left in May. Three roles and three org shapes in three years, all pointed at the same problem. I'll come back to the problem, because it's the most important thing I have to say.

## It should not have worked

None of the machinery makes sense without the conditions that produced it. You've probably read the origin story: Perth, which Cliff Obrecht describes as [the most isolated city in the world](https://techcrunch.com/2022/09/13/canva-moves-beyond-graphic-design-to-launch-a-visual-worksuite/), where he says they didn't know what venture capital was. [More than a hundred investor rejections](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-making-of-canva). Mel [learning to kitesurf](https://www.npr.org/2019/01/24/688299882/canva-melanie-perkins) to get in front of an investor who ran his networking events at kitesurfing retreats. Two things came out of it.

The first is profitability, because when capital is hard to get you build something that doesn't need it. Canva has been [profitable for eight straight years](https://sacra.com/c/canva/) at a growth rate where almost nobody is, and what profitability buys is independence from everyone else's clock. The clearest proof is a bet that almost never comes up when people write about Canva: the company once spent [two years rewriting its entire codebase](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-making-of-canva) and shipped almost no new features while it happened. You can only do that if nobody in the room is running on a runway.

The second is a habit of deriving things from scratch. Being half a day and seven thousand miles from Silicon Valley doesn't make you provincial. It makes you uninterested in calibrating yourself to whatever a room in San Francisco currently considers normal. In 2024 the company closed Canva Create with a rap battle; [fifty million people saw it inside 48 hours](https://www.fastcompany.com/91313954/canva-create-musical-performance) and a decent share of tech Twitter called it cringe. The next year Canva came back bigger: a 42-person cast, a live band, a gospel choir, lyrics drawn from real feature requests. The choice, as it was put internally, was to shrink and play it safe or be unapologetically ourselves.

There is a great deal of costume, and underneath it the hardest-working group of people I've been around. I started calling it laid-back intensity and I still don't have a better phrase. Nobody is performing stress. Everybody is moving extremely fast.

## One percent of the way there

In 2011, before there was a company, Mel made a deck for something called [Canvas Chef](https://nbt.substack.com/p/canva-original-deck). The pitch was that you could describe an idea and have it appear, designed, without knowing anything about design. Fifteen years later she stood on stage at Canva Create and [quoted her own deck back to the room](https://www.forbes.com.au/news/innovation/canva-create-2026-melanie-perkins-unveils-canva-ai-2-0-and-claude-design-deal/) while launching an AI that does exactly that.

![A slide from the original 2011 Canvas Chef pitch deck: an iPad mockup asking "What do you want to chef up?"](/images/posts/canvas-chef-2011.jpg)

*From the original 2011 Canvas Chef deck, via [NBT's archive of the pitch](https://nbt.substack.com/p/canva-original-deck).*

Canva is the only place I've worked where a document written before the company existed still functions as a spec. When thousands of people can predict what the founder would say about a decision, they stop needing to ask her about every one, and she gets to choose where to be involved rather than being the bottleneck everywhere.

It also never lets you arrive. Mel has [said a version of the mantra publicly](https://leaders.com/articles/leaders-stories/melanie-perkins/): a company is still a startup while its dreams are much bigger than its size. The number never moves.

We launched Canva AI, and it went well. Within what felt like no time the conversation had stopped being about an assistant and started being about an agent that does the whole job, which is roughly what we announced a year later as Canva AI 2.0. The target had moved before I'd finished being pleased about hitting the last one. That should be exhausting, and mostly it isn't, because the expansion is genuine. If the objective is to empower the world to design, then the world is large and design is most things, and there is no version of the job where you're finished. Mel holds ambition at that size without ever letting it curdle into fantasy, and I haven't seen anyone else manage it.

## The blank design

Mel is in the details, all of them, and so is Cliff. Paul Graham [gave the pattern a name](https://paulgraham.com/foundermode.html) in 2024, eleven years after Canva started running it, and the conversation his essay kicked off gets stuck on whether a founder should be that involved. That's the boring half. Plenty of founders are deep in the details and produce nothing but chaos. What's worth studying at Canva is what the involvement produces.

On Canva AI 2.0 we were designing memory for the agent. What we brought to the review was a good version of the thing you've already seen elsewhere: the assistant remembers facts about you and states them back as a list. Mel's objection wasn't that it was wrong. It was that it was techie. It was memory the way engineers understand memory, dressed up a bit.

So she opened a blank Canva design and started drawing.

What she prototyped in the room was a library. Not a list of remembered facts, but a shelf of books organized by category: the big events in your life, the campaigns you run, the things that are simply true about you. Then she pushed it into the onboarding, where a single button would read everything you had ever made in Canva and write an About Me document to seed the library on your first day. You can go and look at it. It shipped as the [Memory Library](https://www.canva.com/help/memory-library/), and the first book in it is called About Me. The thing she drew on a blank canvas in a product review made it all the way to launch with its mental model completely intact, which almost never happens to anything. She didn't write a brief or ask for three options. She made the artifact herself, in the product, in front of the people who were going to build it.

Here's the part I've been leaving out. A team had spent weeks on the design we brought into that room. It was good work. Ten minutes later it wasn't the plan anymore.

That happens at Canva more than anywhere else I've worked, and it can be hard on people. Not because anyone's ego is bruised. Because the thing that keeps a team fast is believing that the work they're doing now is the work that ships, and every time the founder redraws something that belief takes a small hit. Do it enough times without care and you get a team that hedges, holds back its best thinking, and waits to find out what the real plan is before committing. That's the real cost of building a company this way, and it isn't the founder who pays it. It's the layers in between.

Canva's answer is mostly a rule: whoever built the thing presents it. Mel wants the person whose hands were on the work in the room, talking, not a director summarizing at three levels of remove. It sounds like meeting etiquette and it isn't. The person whose design got replaced hears the reasoning first-hand, from her, in the moment, and that's most of why a redraw usually lands as energizing rather than demoralizing. When it goes well, people walk out of a review like that with an obviously better idea and the reasons for it. When it goes badly, you've spent some of the team's belief, and everyone sitting between Mel and the team spends the next week earning it back.

## The harness

I started calling the whole apparatus a harness, a word borrowed from working with LLMs: the model makes the call, and the harness is everything around it, the context it's fed, the tools it can reach, the loops that let it see what happened and try again. You don't improve that system by wishing for a bigger model. You improve the harness. Canva has been running that architecture on a person since long before anyone had a word for it. Rituals exist so Mel sees the right work at the right stage. Reviews are structured so she's looking at the real artifact rather than a description of it. Teams are shaped so the surface she touches is the surface where her judgment adds the most, and everything else runs without her.

And when the latency between her judgment and the work gets too high, Canva reorganizes. Not when a leader leaves, not when headcount crosses a number, not because the planning cycle came round again. When the loop gets slow, the org changes shape and the person doesn't. Three roles in three years, all pointed at that one problem, and I never once thought the reorgs were arbitrary.

The bar the harness protects is hers. Before AI 2.0 shipped, Mel came to a review with a twenty-page deck she had built herself by being the product's biggest power user: one issue per slide, down to the hover animation on the Canva AI logo in the sidebar. Our design lead turned that deck into a dedicated polish workstream running for the final months on nothing but end-to-end quality. Twenty slides of complaints became a team. She has the highest craft bar I've worked under, and it produces no fear, because [feedback at Canva is constant and unarmored](https://ethics.org.au/how-the-canva-crew-learned-to-love-feedback/) and nobody believes the standard is about them. She's hardest on her own work.

## Cliff

None of this holds without Cliff. A company organized to move one person's judgment quickly will amplify all of it, so the second voice in the room has to be genuinely independent, and Cliff Obrecht is one of the best operators I have ever seen. He will say the thing he actually thinks in front of everyone, including when it is the opposite of what Mel just said. They are married, they have built a [$40 billion company](https://sacra.com/c/canva/) together, and they will still argue about a feature in front of the team without either of them softening it. The disagreement is the signal. If the team never sees it, they never learn how the decision was actually made.

Three times while I was there, a senior leader left and Cliff took over running their organization himself. Not as a placeholder while a search ran. He actually ran it, on the theory that you can't hire the right person to lead a group until you've been inside its machinery and know what the job really is.

The part I'll miss is the format. A real strategic disagreement would arrive as a considered document laying out his thinking, with screenshots of the examples that got him there. A quality problem would arrive as the screenshot and one line, usually along the lines of: *this is crap, lol.* That is a better way to run a company than it looks. The document tells you he did the work himself before asking you to do more of it. The "lol" tells you it isn't about you. Between them you know within about four seconds exactly where you stand, which is more than most executives manage in a quarter.

## Step two

Canva has what it calls a [two-step plan](https://www.canva.com/step-two/). Step one is to become one of the most valuable companies in the world. Step two is to do the most good it can. Mel worked out early that it isn't a sequence; the steps feed each other, because the mission is a large part of why the best people join and stay. She and Cliff have committed [more than 80% of their wealth](https://www.forbes.com.au/news/leadership/inside-billionaire-canva-cofounders-plan-to-give-away-their-fortune/), including 30% of the company, and the largest thing it funds is a [$150 million commitment](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/give-directly-update-2025/) to hand unconditional cash to people in extreme poverty in Malawi, about the least self-flattering way to give money away that exists. From inside, it changes the arithmetic: every dollar of enterprise value you help create is partly aimed at problems that actually matter. It's why I stayed as long as I did, and I don't think I was unusual.

## Why I left

I was in California. Everyone I worked with was in Sydney. For three years that was manageable. What changed is that the loop got faster: coding agents compressed the distance between an idea and a working version of it far enough that the company started re-centralizing around the speed of its own iteration, and a seventeen-hour gap stops being a scheduling inconvenience and starts being a place where decisions happen without you. The harness ran faster than I could be inside it. That isn't a complaint. It's the strongest evidence I have that the thing I've described here is real.

It's also the shape of what's coming for everyone else. For most of software history the bottleneck was building. That constraint is dissolving, and when a working version of an idea takes an afternoon instead of a quarter, throughput stops being gated on engineering capacity and starts being gated on how fast good judgment can reach the work. Which is the problem Canva has been solving, without meaning to, since 2013. A lot of companies are about to reach for founder mode and get the visible half. The founder in the details, the reviews, the redrawn designs. That half is easy and it mostly produces chaos. The harness is the work, it takes years, and nobody writes about it because it isn't a personality.

What I'm taking with me is mostly two habits. Prototype the idea instead of describing it, because a blank design and ten minutes beat a brief every time. And decide what the money is for before you have any of it. There's no way to install a Melanie Perkins, but the harness can be copied, and so can the ambition.

Mel would say we're one percent of the way there. She was saying it long before I arrived, and she said it again after we shipped the thing we'd spent two years building. For a long time I heard it as a way of keeping people hungry. I don't hear it that way anymore. It's the most accurate sentence anyone at that company says.
